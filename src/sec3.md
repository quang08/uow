Establishing an instrument capable of that distinction begins with the recognition that Orientalism operates within fashion discourse as a system of representation rather than as an accumulation of individual prejudices. Narumi's account of fashion Orientalism, developed through an analysis of the limits of counter-cultural dress, describes a discourse in which Euro-American aesthetic norms are treated as universal while other cultures are rendered exotic, decorative and subordinate, constituting a relation of power rather than a matter of taste (Narumi, 2000). Carrying Said's framework into the sociology of fashion, Kawamura demonstrates that this operation is performed most consistently by fashion journalism, within which ethnicity circulates as a commodity made available for Western consumption (Kawamura, 2005). Locating the instrument in criticism rather than in garments is essential to the present analysis, since the question at issue concerns what Western commentary produced rather than what the designers intended.

Accounting for the mechanics of that reproduction, and for the possibility of challenge, requires two further terms. Drawing on Bhabha's account of mimicry, in which the colonised subject reproduces dominant forms while remaining permanently short of equivalence, Kondo argues that Japanese designers were admitted to the Paris system as innovators yet marked as "not quite/not white", their recognition producing anxiety and their success re-coded through stereotypes of imitation, national grouping and invasion (Kondo, 2010). The accusation of imitation is therefore not an alternative to the structural relation but the discursive surface on which it becomes visible. Supplying the reciprocal term, Kondo's earlier theorisation of counter-Orientalisms describes the means by which racialised subjects contest, redirect and rework the representations imposed upon them, establishing that Orientalist discourse is ambivalent rather than monolithic (Kondo, 1997). These terms yield four operations, traced in what follows across both receptions: the marking of origin, by which a practitioner's work is explained by nationality rather than by practice; the denial of coevalness, Fabian's term for the refusal to grant another culture a present shared with one's own (Fabian, 1983); the framing of the body; and counter-Orientalist pressure.

Nothing downstream needs changing. The instrument can now be invoked by name anywhere without explanation, which is what §6's synthesis sentence and §7's second paragraph were written to depend on.

### One gap in the naming sentence

**"The framing of the body" is the only one of the four given no gloss.** The marking of origin gets a defining clause, the denial of coevalness gets Fabian and a definition, and counter-Orientalist pressure is derived from Kondo (1997) in the preceding sentence. The third is a bare noun phrase.

It is also the operation that carries the most analytical weight downstream: §4's third paragraph, §5's second paragraph and the whole of §6 turn on it, and §6's comparison is conducted at the level of body-relation specifically. A reader meeting it undefined in §3 and then finding it load-bearing in three later sections will have to reconstruct what it means.

A clause of the kind the other three receive would fix it, something on the order of the terms in which commentary describes the relation between garment and wearing body. That addition would also help the overage problem below, since it could replace rather than extend.

### Brief overage

Roughly 380 words against 318, and five citations against four. The second paragraph carries both overages: it runs about 220 words and holds three of the five citations.

Fabian is the only citation that could go without losing a source the essay needs elsewhere, but cutting him would leave the denial of coevalness unattributed, which is worse than being one citation over. The more likely resolution is prose compression in the second paragraph's middle, particularly the sentence beginning "The accusation of imitation is therefore not an alternative", which restates the preceding sentence's point rather than advancing it.
