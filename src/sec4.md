# §4 — the debuts and their reception

**Five paragraphs · ~950 words · 17 citations · two figures held**

*Submitted text below runs continuously, without headings or paragraph labels. The paragraph map is in the notes.*

---

Situating the debuts within the Paris system requires distinguishing the entry event from the reception phase it initiated. Comme des Garçons and Yohji Yamamoto first showed in Paris in April 1981, yet the garments around which Western commentary organised itself were those of Kawakubo's *Holes* collection of autumn-winter 1982-83 (National Gallery of Victoria, n.d.). Across the reception phase, dated here as 1981 to 1985, an atomic and wartime register was available and recurrent, deployed by particular outlets to attribute the collections to national catastrophe rather than to design intention. Kondo records that *Women's Wear Daily*, with other unsympathetic gatekeepers, characterised the black asymmetrical garments as "the Hiroshima bag-lady look", and that Associated Press coverage named Kawakubo the high priestess of the "Jap wrap" (Kondo, 2010, p. 00). English documents a derisive invocation of Pearl Harbour at the moment of the debut, together with the "Rag Picker" label (English, 2011, pp. 38-39). This register was a resource the discourse could reach for rather than a consensus it had arrived at, and it coexisted throughout with the serious critical strand considered below; its operation was to convert authorship into national symptom, eliding two markedly distinct practices into a single catastrophe of national origin.

Establishing what the collections disrupted requires an account of the convention into which they were received. Western dress has long treated the articulated body as the condition of clothing, construing the garment as an instrument for defining, proportioning and displaying a silhouette held to be given in advance (Bartal, 2023, p. 00). The 1980s intensified that convention rather than relaxing it, consolidating a body-conscious norm of padded shoulders, defined waist and emphatic structure, in which a garment's legibility depended upon its correspondence to the figure beneath [§2 source, p. 00]. Koda, writing from within the reception phase, reads the Japanese collections explicitly against this setting, against the conspicuous consumption and the optimism about wealth that characterised the Western 1980s (Koda, 1985, p. 8). The convention at issue is therefore not a neutral background but an active aesthetic programme, and the collections entered a field in which the display of wealth upon a defined body constituted the prevailing measure of fashionability. Read against that convention, the garments propose a relation between cloth and body for which it possesses no vocabulary.

Turning to the objects themselves, the hand-knitted wool jumper perforated with randomly placed holes [**Figure 1**] and the Yamamoto ensemble [**Figure 2**] propose a relation between cloth and body governed by volume, wrapping and the interval between garment and skin (Bartal, 2023, p. 00). Received within the convention described above, such a proposition registers as negation rather than as alternative, and the terminology subsequently imported to manage it carried framing effects of its own, since designating the work deconstruction lent it Western theoretical prestige while assimilating it to a European philosophical genealogy. Gill maintains that certain garments of the period genuinely enact a deconstructive critique of the garment's own conditions (Gill, 1998, p. 00), so the objection concerns the looseness of critical usage rather than any misreading of Kawakubo's practice. At this point mimicry fails to resolve, since the designers are competent within the Paris field yet produce work that field cannot assimilate, which is the anxiety Bhabha describes in the colonised who may mimic perfectly and remain "not quite/ not white" (Bhabha, 1987, as cited by Kondo, 2010, p. 00). That one vocabulary covered two markedly different practices is the national-grouping operation, exemplified in *Vogue*'s retrospective categorisation of the Japanese designers under the language of invasion (Kondo, 2010, p. 00), which establishes that the framing of the body belonged to the same structure as the marking of origin.

Following the terminology imported to manage the garments, the commentary read deliberate formal strategies as deprivation, withholding from the collections the status of modernist practice. Koda names the work an aesthetic of poverty, a formulation which takes the garments seriously as a coherent proposition while locating that proposition in want rather than in intention (Koda, 1985, pp. 5-10). Vinken corrects that naming, holding that the aesthetic is not principally one of poverty, even granting poverty's contribution to the work's provocative impact, but "a negative aesthetic, an examination of our idea of fashion itself" (Vinken, 2023, p. 00). Evans situates the formal logic of the garments within a lineage of modernity rather than exoticism, locating their asymmetry, volume and deliberate incompletion within an avant-garde genealogy to which the criticism of the period declined to admit them (Evans, 2003, p. 00). The disagreement between Koda's contemporaneous naming and Vinken's later correction is itself the evidence, since the denial of coevalness proceeded not principally through hostility but through the reading of intention as damage, an operation which the criticism most sympathetic to the work performed.

Alongside these framings, a documented critical strand read the work as a conceptual intervention and recorded the derisive register as one position among several. Morris, reviewing the debut, described Kawakubo as constantly poking beyond the borders of established fashion (Morris, 1981, as cited by Marra-Alvarez, 2010, p. 00), while recording two years later that journalists of the period had assigned the collections labels such as ragged chic and the bag-lady look (Morris, 1983, as cited by Marra-Alvarez, 2010, p. 00), which indicates a field divided from within rather than a consensus arrived at. The structure of that division constitutes the finding. Duka relocates Yamamoto's work into London street fashion while reaching for the shapes of ancient Japan in the same sentence (Duka, 1982, as cited by Marra-Alvarez, 2010, p. 00), and Koda traces the look to Westwood and McLaren rather than to any Japanese origin, yet grounds the aesthetic in a sensibility derived from Zen (Koda, 1985, p. 7). Kawakubo later characterised the debut's aggressive style as a game intended to establish the house's position (Séguret, 1988, p. 00), though that remark postdates the debut by roughly seven years and evidences retrospective self-positioning rather than contemporaneous intent. Assessed as a question of degree, reproduction was dominant, challenge is locatable in what the garments proposed, in the designers' own positioning and in a documented critical strand, and the counter-pressure was structured by the frame it contested, since the critics who refused the national-origin explanation reached for Zen and ancient Japan to replace it. Ambivalence ran through individual critics rather than between camps. Establishing whether this reception was continuous with a longer tradition requires the same criteria to be applied to an earlier object and the commentary it attracted.
