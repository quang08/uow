Turning from the reception to the objects themselves, the material records permit a comparison the commentary never undertook. The Paris garments are recorded in terms that describe construction rather than effect: colour reduced throughout to black, which removes colour as a decorative variable; a jumper hand-knitted in stocking stitch rather than tailored, its perforations worked at random as a designed element rather than introduced as damage (Victoria and Albert Museum, n.d.); and an ensemble produced by straight cutting into oversized volume, whose deliberately created holes form conventionalised floral patterns and whose every hole edge is elaborately finished, so that the well-worn appearance it presents is an achievement of labour rather than a condition of want (Kyoto Costume Institute, n.d.). In both, volume displaces the outline and an interval is opened between cloth and skin, so that the body is inferred rather than constructed, which is the structural rather than surface logic Fukai distinguishes (Fukai, 1996, p. 00). The Rokumeikan survives differently. Chikanobu's triptych of 1887 [**Figure 5**], depicting a charity bazaar held at the hall, shows a bustle and a defined waist, a structured skirt with trimming, hat and boots, the figure held upright in the posture the garment requires, and the bodies arranged within a Western interior (Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, n.d.). There the silhouette is imposed and the body conforms to it. That the Paris collections survive as garments while the Rokumeikan survives chiefly as image is itself a fact about which bodies the record preserves and in what form, and it sets the comparison at the level of silhouette and the relation between garment and body rather than at the level of construction. At that level the two are opposite.

Setting the two records side by side establishes the finding toward which the preceding sections have been building, an opposition the essay constructs by juxtaposition, since neither object was made against the other. The marking of origin, the denial of coevalness, the framing of the body and the counter-Orientalist pressure each ran upon both objects with equal force, yet the objects are opposite in precisely the respect the criticism claimed to be judging, since one imposes a silhouette upon the body and the other withdraws from it. If the same operations applied with equal force to garments opposite in their relation to the body, those operations were not responses to the garments. The reading was invariant to its object, and what was being read was origin. That invariance accounts for what would otherwise be anomalous in either reception considered alone: that a single vocabulary could cover two designers of markedly different practice in 1981, and that a faultlessly correct silhouette and a deliberately unfinished one could attract the same charge of inauthenticity ninety-five years apart. It accounts too for why the counter-pressure in both cases originated with the people the commentary described and operated inside the frame rather than outside it, since a reading indifferent to its object cannot be corrected by the production of a better object (Kondo, 2010, p. 00). What the Western criticism of these two moments was reading was never the garment in front of it.
