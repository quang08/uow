# Reflective Commentary

## Authorial Intention

The relationship between high-fashion design and historical fine art has traditionally been studied as a problem of aesthetic homage. Scholars examining Vivienne Westwood's 1990 'Portrait' collection have typically focused on how she translated 18th-century Rococo paintings into modern couture, treating her garments as visual citations of historical elegance.

However, past research has mostly treated these historical references as stylistic decoration, assuming that Westwood’s use of François Boucher was a simple exercise in romantic nostalgia.

My intention is to test that assumption. Through an intertextual and material analysis linking 18th-century French painting with 1980s punk subculture, I set out to establish whether Westwood’s historical borrowings operated as passive cultural pastiche or as active political interventions that challenged historical constructs of gender, class, and institutional power. The question I ask is to what extent did Vivienne Westwood use 18th-century artworks alongside punk bricolage to inform her anti-establishment designs?

To do this I construct a dual-layered historical and theoretical framework.

---

## I. Constructing a Purpose-Built Instrument

Rather than relying on description, I assemble an analytical instrument drawing on Dick Hebdige’s theory of *bricolage*, combined with historical analyses of 18th-century French sociopolitics and feminist critiques of art and dress. It consists of four operations:

1. **Subcultural Bricolage:** analyzing how disparate objects and symbols are dismantled and reassembled to generate an anarchical or oppositional tone.


2. **Contextual Misplacement:** observing how moving a symbol (such as an elite Rococo painting or a suppressed textile like tartan) into an unexpected context alters its discourse.


3. **Framing of Gender and the Body:** evaluating how historical undergarments (like the corset) and silhouette manipulation contest patriarchal controls over the female body.


4. **Institutional Subversion:** tracking how the appropriation of high-art and political motifs parodies and belittles established authorities.



---

## II. Isolating the Variable of Historical Appropriation

I pair Westwood's late 20th-century punk framework with the sociopolitical climate of 18th-century France, applying the analytical operations across both domains.

* **18th-Century Context.** François Boucher's paintings and subjects (such as Madame de Pompadour) faced fierce criticism from contemporaries like La Font de Saint-Yenne for challenging traditional artistic hierarchies, embracing cosmetic artifice, and bypassing moral constraints.


* **1990 'Portrait' Collection.** Westwood printed Boucher’s erotically charged *Daphnis and Chloe* directly onto synthetic corsets, exposing undergarments as outer garments and juxtaposing rococo drapery with structured, masculine tailoring and tartan.



By placing these two moments side-by-side, the analysis demonstrates that Westwood was not merely romanticizing the 18th century; she was weaponizing its internal scandals. The historical artworks functioned as political vehicles that mirrored the subversive, anti-establishment posture of punk.

---

## III. Reframing the Function of Historical Inspiration

I shift attention from fashion as a superficial surface of trend toward fashion as a medium of historical and political commentary.

* **Subversive Continuity:** Just as punk fashion reflected the economic and political strife of post-war Britain by wearing social "ugliness" and distress, Westwood’s use of 18th-century motifs targeted ignorance toward hierarchy, class, and institutional restraint.


* **Reclaiming the Erotic and Decried:** By recontextualizing the corset—historically viewed as a coercive patriarchal instrument—Westwood transformed it from a tool of internal torture into an outward expression of female autonomy and sexual power.



---

## IV. Complicating the Concept of Designer Agency

I resist the binary that views historical inspiration as a direct path to liberation. Drawing on critical perspectives of subculture, I argue that Westwood's challenge was deeply ambivalent and operated strictly from within the structural limits of fashion and art history.

* **The Ambivalence of the Body:** While the display of the corset and Rococo prints challenged modesty, it simultaneously navigated the fine line between celebrating female sexuality and confronting the historical objectification embedded within those exact visual tropes.


* **Negotiation Within the System:** Westwood’s subversion did not destroy the fashion or museum system; rather, it renegotiated elite historical symbols (frequenting the Wallace Collection) from within the commercial framework of high-end *prêt-à-porter*.



---

## Answering the Research Question

To what extent did Vivienne Westwood use 18th-century artworks alongside punk bricolage to inform her anti-establishment designs?

The evidence supports an answer that sees Westwood using historical art not as nostalgic ornament, but as a critical instrument of *bricolage*. By weaponizing Boucher’s controversial 18th-century portraits through unexpected placements on structured corsetry and juxtaposing them with rebellious tartans and masculine tailoring, Westwood systematically dismantled conventional boundaries of gender, class, and hierarchy. Her design practice proved that historical reference could be structurally repurposed to articulate contemporary political dissent from within the heart of the cultural establishment.
