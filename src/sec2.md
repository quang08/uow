Situating the designers within the history that preceded them requires beginning well before 1981. Japan's first sustained encounter with Western dress came in the Meiji period, when Western clothing was adopted as an instrument of modernisation and the dress of Japanese bodies became, for the first time, a question the West read as a question about Japanese modernity (Cambridge, 2011, p. 00). The postwar decades then established the material conditions from which the designers emerged. Donzé and Fujioka show that the Japanese apparel industry developed in close interaction with textile firms, producing a fashion system organised around production and technology rather than around brand and creation, which is to say that Japanese industrial modernity in clothing was a matter of record rather than of Western perception (Donzé and Fujioka, 2021, p. 00). That industrial base coincided with a broader economic ascendancy, and by the late 1970s a formerly subordinate Japan had become an economic rival, its goods known for precisely the quality the immediate postwar period had denied them (Kondo, 2010, p. 00). The rivalry generated its own vocabulary, and Western coverage of the trade conflict deployed tropes of domination, invasion and unfair practice (Kondo, 2010, p. 00), while the anti-Japanese sentiment accompanying that conflict circulated widely enough to reach the fashion press (English, 2011, p. 00). Alongside it ran the formation Morley and Robins name techno-orientalism, in which Japan is construed as technologically advanced yet culturally inscrutable, modern in its capacities and alien in its nature (Morley and Robins, 1995, p. 00), a construction to which Japanese dress, read through the uniform and the standardised body, was particularly available (Cambridge, 2011, p. 00). The vocabulary Western commentary brought to the collections of the early 1980s was therefore not produced by them. Imitation, invasion, national grouping and inscrutability were already in circulation, supplied by a political and economic history in which Japanese success had become a source of Western anxiety. That the resulting habit of explanation is itself open to objection has been established from outside fashion studies, since Donzé and Fujioka find that accounts of Japanese fashion have characteristically reached for ethnic explanation, stressing street fashion and star designers in Paris, and that such accounts cannot explain the industry's actual trajectory (Donzé and Fujioka, 2021, p. 00). The garments therefore arrived into a discourse whose terms were waiting for them.

Within Paris, those inherited terms met a particular institution and a particular dominant aesthetic. The Paris fashion system of the period was governed by the Chambre Syndicale, which regulated the distinction between haute couture and prêt-à-porter, administered the showing calendar and determined which houses appeared within it, so that admission to the collections was an institutional act rather than a matter of reputation (Kawamura, 2004, p. 00). Gatekeeping was performed in turn by the press and the buyers, whose attendance and coverage converted a showing into a position within the field (Kawamura, 2004, p. 00). The aesthetic those gatekeepers were trained upon was the most insistently body-defining of the postwar era. Steele's survey of the period describes the dominant 1980s silhouette as a structure of padded shoulders, defined waist and emphatic line (Steele, 1997, p. 00), and Arnold reads that silhouette as a moral and economic statement, the decade's confidence written upon the body in a form equating visible structure with visible success (Arnold, 2001, p. 00). Entwistle's analysis of power dressing, addressed to the professional woman rather than to the decade at large, establishes that such dress operated as a discourse instructing its wearers to act upon their bodies, which makes the convention an active programme rather than a neutral background (Entwistle, 2000, p. 00). Koda, writing from within the period, locates the conspicuous consumption and the optimism about wealth which made this the prevailing measure of fashionability (Koda, 1985, p. 8). Into that setting the Japanese designers arrived by a route the system had not anticipated, showing within the prêt-à-porter calendar and attracting an early response that was limited and mixed rather than uniformly hostile (Marra-Alvarez, 2010, p. 00). The reception which followed was produced by the collision of three things: an institution with the authority to admit and to withhold, a dominant aesthetic organised around the articulated body, and the inheritance of anxiety described above. The question that reception poses is therefore not whether the commentary was hostile or admiring, but whether it reproduced or challenged the representations it had inherited, a distinction the commentary's own vocabulary cannot be relied upon to make.

### Entwistle checked, and the attribution split had to change

**Your suspicion was right.** Entwistle's power-dressing material is discursive and sociological, not descriptive. *The Fashioned Body* is subtitled *Fashion, Dress and Modern Social Theory* and surveys theory; her power-dressing analysis treats it as a discourse on the body, sexuality, gender performance and power, examining how that discourse constructs women's bodies as obstacles to be managed and calls on career women to act upon themselves as a project of the self. She does not supply a costume-historical account of the decade's silhouette.

There is a second problem with her carrying the description. Her material addresses **women's professional dress specifically**, which is narrower than §4's claim about the decade's dominant convention. Attributing a general silhouette claim to her would have overrun her scope in the same way the trade-deficit clause overran Kondo.
