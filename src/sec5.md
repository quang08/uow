# §5 — the Rokumeikan ball gown and its reception

**Three paragraphs · ~665 words · 11 citations · one figure held**

*Submitted text below runs continuously, without headings or paragraph labels. The paragraph map is in the notes.*

---

Turning to an earlier object, the Western-style ball gown worn by Japanese women at the Rokumeikan in Tokyo between 1883 and 1889 [**Figure 4**] entered a Western system of dress not as a borrowing of motifs but as a wholesale adoption, undertaken at a state reception hall built to demonstrate Japanese fitness for diplomatic parity (Cambridge, 2011, p. 00). Loti, attending the Emperor's birthday ball there in November 1886, organised his account around the charge of imitation, locating the interest of the spectacle in its failure to convince rather than in what it represented, and describing his hosts as "unrivaled in feats of dexterity", a formulation which concedes accomplishment while withholding authorship (Loti, 1889, p. 00). The account's recourse to a simian comparison makes the racial marking explicit, and the dexterity it concedes is the dexterity of the copyist. Loti was a travel writer rather than a commentator on dress, a genre difference worth stating plainly, since the operations his account performs are those fashion criticism would perform on Japanese designers a century later. The marking of the wearers by nationality and by race, together with the reduction of their adoption of Western dress to mimicry, reproduces the marking of origin in a setting where the garments themselves were faultlessly correct.

Establishing what produced that commentary requires attention to the political asymmetry in which the Rokumeikan was built. Westernisation at the hall was deliberate state policy pursued in the context of treaty revision, which constitutes the ball gown a political instrument of the Meiji project of modernity rather than a fashion preference, and which placed its wearers in a position where the adoption of Western forms was simultaneously necessary and disallowed (Cambridge, 2011, p. 00). Loti registers that asymmetry upon the body, attending minutely to fit, posture and movement, and finding the Japanese body unsuited to the garment rather than the garment unsuited to the body (Loti, 1889, p. 00). The repertoire available to him for describing an Oriental body in Western dress supplied the terms of that judgement in advance (Martin & Koda, 1994, p. 00). The operation is the one performed upon the Paris collections, running in the opposite direction: in 1981 Japanese designers proposed a relation between cloth and body for which the Western convention possessed no vocabulary, whereas in 1886 Japanese women adopted that convention exactly and were found wanting within it. Admission without equivalence governs both cases, which establishes the framing of the body as continuous across the two receptions rather than particular to either.

Underlying that repertoire is a structure which positions authentic Japan as perpetually in the act of disappearing. Loti locates his subject's reality not in the room he is describing but in the kimono she will resume once the evening ends, placing her authenticity in what she has set aside and denying her any present of her own (Loti, 1889, p. 00). The denial at issue is of contemporaneity rather than of modernity in general, and it operates through the structure Ivy names as a vanishing, an ongoing process in which the authentic is always in the act of being lost and never available in the present (Ivy, 1995, p. 00). Yet the project the wearers were participating in was a Japanese one, which places self-orientalisation within the same frame and attracts the double indictment Narumi describes (Narumi, 2000, p. 00). It generated its own rewriting as well, since Akutagawa's 'Butōkai' of 1920 retells Loti's evening from the viewpoint of one of the women he had described, a counter-Orientalism which Rosenfeld shows to be ambivalent in its own right, resentful of Loti's disdain while intimating Japan's own imperial ambitions (Rosenfeld, 2000, pp. 53-63). Assessed as the Paris reception was, reproduction was dominant and the counter-pressure was structured by the frame it contested. The 1980s commentary therefore did not invent its vocabulary of imitation, national grouping and authentic tradition but inherited a structure already operating upon Japanese dress a century earlier, and in both receptions the counter-pressure originated with the people the commentary described.
