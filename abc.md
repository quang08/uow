## 2. The two artifacts, and the crucial difference from our plan

**Artifact 1** is not "the Portrait collection" in the abstract. It is one garment: the "Statue of Liberty" corset, synthetic blend, Boucher's *Daphnis and Chloe* printed on the front panel, gold and mauve classical motifs on the straps, gold lycra sides, **V&A accession O71199**.

**Artifact 2** is not "eighteenth-century art" in the abstract. It is two paintings: *Daphnis and Chloe* (Wallace Collection) and *Pompadour at Her Toilette* (Harvard Art Museums, object 303561).

All three are specific, locatable objects with museum records and accession URLs.

**More importantly, §5 is not background.** It analyses Artifact 2 *and the criticism Boucher attracted in his own era*: La Font de Saint-Yenne's attacks on his subject matter, the charge of frivolity and bad taste, the dévot faction's campaign against Pompadour, the political reading of the portrait. The comparison the essay builds is reception-to-reception, not object-to-context. Boucher provoked controversy in the 1740s as Westwood provoked it in 1990, and the parallel is between the two bodies of hostile criticism.

**Our §5 is currently planned as a discursive structure**: Japonism, the Orientalist tradition in Western dress, the vanishing. That is context, not a second case, and it is why criterion (iv) had nowhere to go. Our RQ is explicitly about *criticism*, so a §5 that analyses discourse in general rather than a specific object and its specific reception fails the symmetry the comparison depends on.

## 3. Citation practice

### Source types in the bibliography

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| University press monographs | Davis (Chicago), Steele (Yale), Leblanc (Rutgers), Hufton (Toronto), Phillippy (Johns Hopkins) |
| Academic trade presses | Hebdige and Hauser (Routledge), Geczy & Karaminas, Sklar, Faiers (Bloomsbury) |
| Research-institute publications | Hyde, and Hyde & Ledbury (Getty Research Institute) |
| Museum publications | Bolton (Metropolitan Museum), Wilcox (V&A) |
| Peer-reviewed journals | Linton (*History of European Ideas*, with DOI), Gordon (*Eighteenth-Century Studies*, JSTOR), Nijhawan (Tulane) |
| Museum object records | V&A, Wallace Collection, Harvard Art Museums |
| Journalism and institutional web | BBC, Dazed, National Geographic, National Trust for Scotland, Yale Books blog |

**No theses. No dissertations. No coursework. No unpublished student writing of any kind.**

Journalism appears, but only for factual or contextual colour (the history of tartan, the 1770 Act, a curator quoted in a feature), never as the authority for an analytical claim. The analytical weight sits entirely on monographs, research-institute volumes and peer-reviewed articles.

### Our exposure

Our pool currently contains four items that fail this standard, two of them load-bearing:

| ID | What it is | Current role |
|---|---|---|
| C17 | Undergraduate coursework hosted on a college archive | Was carrying §2 debut conditions, §5 World's Fairs, and a key quotation |
| C18 | Courtauld BA assessed essay | Sole source for both anchor garments and the Orientalising-gaze argument in P11 and P16 |
| B6 Querolo | Repository item, degree level unconfirmed | Sole source for the Japanese body in P14 |
| C19 Uboldi | Politecnico di Milano master's thesis | Reprise only in §6 |

Measured against the model the professor has recommended, this is the single largest quality problem in the plan. The remedy is not to discard them but to demote all four to locating aids: they point to primary sources and corroborate arguments made elsewhere, and they are not cited as authorities. C18's value is that it identifies two garments and makes an argument also available in peer-reviewed form; C17's value is that it led to the Séguret interview.

### Relay citations

The reference uses explicit relay form throughout: Clarke cited through Hebdige, La Font de Saint-Yenne through Hyde, Levine through Davis, Benjamin through Rocamora and Smelik, a curator through a journalist. It gives the original author, the original date, and the intermediary with a page.

This confirms Option B and improves on it. **Adopt the reference's explicit form**: `(Bhabha, 1987, as cited by Kondo, 2010, p. 00)` rather than naming Bhabha in prose alone. It is more honest and it is evidently the house convention expected.

### Format

Harvard author-date with page numbers, parentheticals inside the sentence, alphabetical bibliography with "Available at: … (Accessed: date)" for online sources. This confirms the decision already recorded.

### Quotation

The reference uses two indented block quotes of roughly 40 to 60 words, each carrying a key theoretical formulation, plus frequent short quoted phrases of two to ten words. Our current rule 17 forbids any quotation past a single clause, which is stricter than the model. Relaxing it to permit **one block quote in §3**, for the instrument's central formulation, would match the reference. Everything else stays short.

## 4. Figures

Five figures, each with a numbered caption giving subject, source, URL and access date. Our plan has no figure programme at all, which is a gap.

Note what the figures do: **the caption carries the material description, which frees the prose to analyse rather than describe.** The corset's fabric, print placement and strap detailing are all in the Figure 3 caption, taken from the V&A record, so the paragraph can go straight to what the juxtaposition means.

This resolves the C18 provenance problem cleanly. Put the catalogued material description in the figure caption, sourced to the museum record, and let the prose make the argument.

## 5. What to change in our plan

**1. Give §5 a specific artifact and its specific reception.** Replace "Japonism and Orientalism in Western dress" as a discursive survey with one or two located nineteenth-century objects and the Western commentary they attracted. Candidates: a Liberty & Co. or Babani export kimono or kimono-derived tea gown; a Worth or Poiret japoniste garment; a Japanese export textile shown at an Exposition Universelle. Martin & Koda, Fukai and the V&A and KCI collections are the routes to a documented example.

This change does three things at once. It makes the comparison reception-to-reception, as the RQ requires. It gives criterion (iv) an object to work on, since a documented export piece carries Japanese production decisions and therefore agency. And it removes the dependence on C17's World's Fairs material.

**2. Demote all four student sources to locating aids**, and take every physical description from a museum record.

**3. Adopt the explicit relay form** for Bhabha, Said, Derrida and Sudjic.

**4. Build the figure programme in now**, before drafting, since the captions absorb descriptive work the paragraphs would otherwise carry and the word budget assumes they do.

**5. Keep the interim-verdict close.** The reference's RQ asks *how*; ours asks *to what extent*. Its sections close by synthesising; ours must close by calibrating. §4 and §5 each end with a stated degree of reproduction and challenge, not just a synthesis.

**6. Allow one block quote in §3**, for the instrument's central formulation, matching the model.

## 6. Revised §4 and §5

### §4 — Artifact 1: the debuts and their reception (880w, P9–P12, ~10 citations)

Unchanged in architecture. Three adjustments: Figures 1 and 2 carry the garment descriptions; C18 appears only where the Orientalising-gaze argument is corroborated by a peer-reviewed source; the case period is stated as 1981–85 in P9.

- **P9** Critical vocabulary, with contemporaneous press distinguished from retrospective characterisation
- **P10** Modernity denied: formal strategies read as damage, poverty, mourning
- **P11** The body and the "deconstruction" label, with Figures 1 and 2
- **P12** Counter-pressure: the minority reception, and Kawakubo's retrospective framing via Séguret

### §5 — Artifact 2: a Japonisme object and its reception (620w, P13–P15, ~10 citations)

- **P13** The object introduced and described, with its figure: what it is, who made it, for which market, how it was presented. The Western commentary it attracted, and the vocabulary that commentary used.
- **P14** Criteria (i) and (iii) applied: how the commentary marked it nationally, and how it framed the body wearing it. Martin & Koda for the Orientalist repertoire, Fukai for the kimono's reception.
- **P15** Criteria (ii) and (iv) applied: the denial of contemporaneity, with Ivy's vanishing; and Japanese agency in the object's production and presentation, which is self-orientalisation and connects to Narumi's double indictment. Closes with the interim verdict and the structural continuity with §4.

This is the version that earns the section's place. The abstract version would have read as background with a comparison asserted rather than demonstrated.
