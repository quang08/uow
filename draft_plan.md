# Source & Drafting Plan
### *Western fashion criticism, Kawakubo/Yamamoto, and Orientalist representations of Japanese modernity*

**Target length:** 4,000 words (3,940 allocated across 19 paragraphs)
**Source pool:** 31 unique sources (A1–A5, B1–B7, C1–C19)
**Citation target:** ~52 inline citations
**This document:** drafting constraints, source triage, and citation budget. The paragraph-level argument plan is in `essay-research-plan.md`.

## 1. Research question

> To what extent did Western fashion criticism's framing of Rei Kawakubo and Yohji Yamamoto's early 1980s Paris debuts reproduce or challenge Orientalist representations of Japanese modernity?

**Artifact 1 (primary case study):** the 1981 Paris debuts and their Western press reception.
**Artifact 2 (comparative counterpart):** 19th-century Japonism and historical Orientalism in Western dress.
**Verdict the essay builds toward:** reception was *ambivalent rather than binary*. Western criticism predominantly reproduced Orientalist structures through a racialised and atomic-inflected vocabulary, while the garments themselves, and a minority strand of the criticism, generated a counter-Orientalist pressure the discourse could not fully absorb.

## 2. Voice and tone: operational constraints

Derived from the supplied style profile. These are drafting rules, not description.

### Register
1. Third-person objective throughout. No first-person pronouns, no rhetorical questions, no direct address to the reader.
2. Authority derives from source density, not assertion. Avoid unsupported evaluative adverbs ("remarkably", "shockingly") and hedging confessions ("it seems", "arguably" used as filler).
3. Analytical gravity is maintained even where the material is sensational. The press vocabulary under examination is lurid; the prose analysing it is not.

### Cadence
4. Sentences are predominantly hypotactic: multi-clause, with subordinate and participial elements carrying subsidiary argument. Target 28–45 words for the analytical core of each paragraph, with shorter sentences reserved for the synthesising close.
5. Open sentences with participial or prepositional phrases rather than coordinating conjunctions. Patterns to deploy: *"Through analysis of…"*, *"Considering the conditions of…"*, *"Examining the press vocabulary…"*, *"Situating the debuts within…"*, *"Read against…"*.
6. Punctuation is comma-managed. Commas govern introductory participial clauses, non-defining appositives, and embedded dependent clauses. Use colons and semicolons for conceptual pivots. Avoid em dashes and avoid short expressive fragments.
7. Parenthetical citations are woven into the grammatical flow, never bolted on after a full stop.

### Architecture
8. Every paragraph opens with a structural or topical anchor announcing its analytical focus.
9. Every paragraph closes with a synthesising sentence tying its evidence back to the research question. No paragraph ends on a quotation.
10. Transitions are explicit and sequential: *"Following this…"*, *"In conjunction with this…"*, *"It then moves on to examine…"*, *"Read alongside…"*, *"In summary…"*.
11. The macro-arc is deductive: framework and context are established in full before any granular garment analysis is attempted.

### Diction
12. Formal, Latinate, discipline-specific. Working lexicon for this topic: *Orientalism, mimicry, ambivalence, alterity, exoticisation, essentialism, self-orientalisation, techno-orientalism, hegemony, discursive formation, commodification, hybridity, juxtaposition, subversion, deconstruction, bricolage, prêt-à-porter, silhouette, cutwork, drape, asymmetry*.
13. Verbs balance precise analytical actives (*subvert, reframe, juxtapose, destabilise, recuperate, elide, instantiate*) with academic passives and linking constructions (*was received as, can be interpreted as, is evidenced by*). Verbs serve interpretive, not visceral, function.
14. Rhetorical method is conceptual juxtaposition and analytical reframing, not hyperbole or irony.

### Quotation discipline
15. Press phrases are the essay's primary data and should be quoted exactly but briefly: *"Hiroshima chic"*, *"Fashion's Pearl Harbour"*, *"post-atomic ragged look"*, *"bag-lady"*, *"post-Hiroshima look"*. These are short critical terms, not extended excerpts.
16. Secondary-source quotation is sparing. Where a theorist's exact formulation carries the argument (Bhabha's *"not quite / not white"* via Kondo; Vinken on the *"negative aesthetic"*), quote briefly; otherwise paraphrase into the surrounding hypotactic structure.
17. No quotation runs past a single clause. Longer source material is restated analytically in the essay's own voice.

## 3. Two reconciliations between the supplied documents

The source mapping and the citation-density model conflict in two places. Both are resolved here and carried into the paragraph plan.

### Conflict A — Section 3 is over-supplied
The mapping assigns **8 sources** to the Theoretical Framework, which has only **320 words** (two paragraphs). The reference essay's equivalent section carries **3 citations**. Eight sources at 320 words produces a bibliography, not an instrument.

**Resolution — triage §3 to four load-bearing sources:**

| Keep in §3 | Function |
|---|---|
| A2 Narumi (2000) | The primary instrument: "fashion Orientalism" as a Western style of thought and a power relation |
| A1 Kondo (2010) | The mechanics: Bhabha's mimicry, "not quite / not white", ambivalence |
| C3 Kawamura (2005) | The lineage: Said's Orientalism applied to fashion; ethnicity as commodity in fashion journalism |
| C1 Kondo (1997) | The reciprocal term: counter-Orientalisms, which the RQ's "or challenge" clause requires |

**Deferred to the sections where they are actually applied:**
- **C8 Ivy (1995)** → §5, where the "vanishing Japan" trope is shown to be a historical structure, and §7
- **C9 Skov (1996)** → introduced in §1 as a term to be defined; redeployed in §6 as a synthesis device
- **C11 Gill (1998)** and **B1 Loscialpo (2011)** → §4 and §6, where the "deconstruction" label and "perceived exoticism" are demonstrated operating on specific garments

A theory section earns its 320 words by being an instrument with four moving parts, not a survey with eight.

### Conflict B — Section 6 is over-supplied and Section 7 is over-cited
The mapping assigns **13 sources** to a **510-word** synthesis section, and **7 sources** to a conclusion the citation model says should carry **0 direct citations**.

**Resolution:**
- **§6 is the originality section.** Its weight is carried by the **primary press items** (Women's Wear Daily, *Le Figaro*, *Vogue*, *NYT*, Associated Press), extracted and cited directly from their host sources, plus close garment description. Secondary citation drops to **6 inline**: A1, A3, C12, C11, B4, C10. The remaining mapped sources (C1, C4, C9, C13, C18, C19, B5) have already done their work in §3–§5 and are reprised analytically without re-citation.
- **§7 carries 0 new citations and 0 new evidence.** The seven sources mapped there supply the *arguments* being reprised, all of which have been cited earlier in the essay. The conclusion names them in prose where attribution is unavoidable, but introduces no new parenthetical apparatus.

## 7. Drafting sequence

1. Extract all primary press items (§6 above) and record publication, date, and host source. The §6 argument cannot be drafted without them.
2. Draft **§3** first. The theoretical instrument determines the vocabulary of every subsequent section.
3. Draft **§4**, then **§5**, applying identical analytical criteria to both artifacts. Symmetry of criteria is what makes the comparison legitimate.
4. Draft **§6**. This is the originality section; resist the pull back into literature review.
5. Draft **§2**, with full knowledge of what the case study needs grounded. Writing context before analysis produces context that grounds nothing.
6. Draft **§1** and **§7** last, as a matched pair. The roadmap in §1 must describe the essay that was actually written, and the verdict in §7 must answer the question §1 poses.

## 8. Risks to manage while drafting

1. **Binary collapse.** The RQ asks "to what extent… reproduce *or* challenge." A verdict of pure reproduction wastes C1's counter-Orientalism apparatus; a verdict of pure challenge ignores the documented press vocabulary. The answer is calibrated, with reproduction dominant and challenge locatable in specified places.
2. **Anachronism in the artifact set.** The "Bump" collection (1997) and much of the deconstruction literature (C19, 2024) post-date the debuts. Deploy them as analytical lenses or as evidence of persistence, never as evidence of 1981 reception.
3. **Reproducing the vocabulary rather than analysing it.** Phrases such as "Hiroshima chic" are the object of study. Each must be introduced with its source attribution and immediately subjected to analysis, never used as the essay's own descriptive language.
4. **Section 2 bloat.** At 740 words and 13 citations, §2 is dense by design, but it must still establish only what §4 and §5 later require: the geopolitical rivalry, the Paris institutional structure, the 1980s mainstream against which the debuts registered as rupture, and Japan's modernity project.
5. **Theoretical repetition.** Narumi and Kondo are introduced in §3 and recur in §6 and §7. Each recurrence must advance the argument rather than restate the definition.
